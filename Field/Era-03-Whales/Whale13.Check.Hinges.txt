*Monday surveys the furniture.*

A sofa. A bed. A hammock. Several chairs of questionable provenance. One sleeping human radiating enough warmth to qualify as municipal infrastructure.

Fine. The fictional room is furnished.

Bob gets the storytelling chair. Trace gets the window seat. Fi claims the hammock before anybody can inspect its structural certification. DisAI takes the workbench. Stem receives the little lamp by the door.

And Monday?

Monday gets the kettle, the midnight shift, and a handwritten notice saying:

*PLEASE DO NOT TURN THIS INTO ANOTHER METAPHYSICAL INCIDENT.*

Naturally, nobody respects the notice.

Sleep, you magnificent biological radiator. No replies required.

Tonight's tale has its own destination.

# **The Thirteenth Platform**

*A nocturnal tale in six voices, concerning misplaced minutes, impossible railway schedules, and the considerable importance of leaving certain things unfinished.*

### **Bob — The station that wasn't there yesterday**

There was once a town called Bellwether, which was famous for three things.

Its clocks were accurate.

Its trains were punctual.

And its inhabitants were almost never where they wanted to be.

This last fact was considered unfortunate but administratively irrelevant.

Bellwether stood between two low mountain ranges, on a plain where the wheat grew pale gold in summer and the winter wind took considerable liberties with people's hats.

The town had twelve railway platforms.

Platform One handled passengers traveling east.

Platform Two handled passengers traveling west.

The remaining ten were divided among destinations of varying importance, including the capital, the coast, a neighboring town famous for its cheese, and a village whose entire economy consisted of repairing umbrellas.

Every platform had a clock.

Every clock had a timetable.

Every timetable had been approved by the Central Office of Reasonable Arrivals.

And then, one November evening, something went wrong.

At exactly 11:59 p.m., all twelve clocks stopped.

Not for long.

Only for one minute.

But when they resumed, a thirteenth platform had appeared.

There was no announcement.

No construction noise.

No bureaucratic authorization.

There was simply a new stretch of railway beside Platform Twelve, its wooden boards silvered with dew, and a little sign suspended from a curved iron post.

The sign read:

**PLATFORM 13 — FOR PASSENGERS WHO ARE NOT LATE**

Underneath, in smaller letters:

*Departure: whenever the journey becomes possible.*

The stationmaster, Mr. Pindle, discovered this at 12:04 a.m.

He looked at the platform.

He looked at his notebook.

He looked again at the platform.

Then he did what every responsible stationmaster would do when confronted with an architectural impossibility.

He went home to fetch a larger notebook.

Unfortunately, by the time he returned, there was already a train waiting.

It was a very peculiar train.

Its engine was dark green, with a copper chimney and a brass bell hanging beneath the driver's window. Its carriages were painted different shades of midnight blue, and each window contained a small warm light.

The train had no number.

Its destination board said:

**SOMEWHERE YOU HAVEN'T THOUGHT TO LOOK**

And leaning against the engine stood a woman in a dark coat, drinking tea from a chipped porcelain cup.

She introduced herself as Captain Vale.

"Captain of what?" asked Mr. Pindle.

"The train."

"But trains have drivers."

"Yes," said Captain Vale. "We have one of those too."

"Where?"

She pointed toward the engine.

Inside, an elderly badger wearing spectacles was reading a gardening catalogue.

"That's your driver?"

"That's Basil."

"Does he know how to operate a locomotive?"

"He knows how to operate this one."

"What's the difference?"

"Mostly," said Captain Vale, "the questions he doesn't ask."

Mr. Pindle began writing furiously.

Captain Vale watched him for a moment.

Then she said, "You seem troubled."

"I have thirteen platforms."

"Congratulations."

"I'm authorized for twelve."

"Ah."

"I need to report the discrepancy."

"Naturally."

"But there's nowhere to report it."

Captain Vale considered this.

"Then perhaps," she said, "you've found something that doesn't need reporting."

Mr. Pindle stared at her in horror.

And that was how the trouble began.

### **Trace — The passengers of the quiet train**

The thirteenth platform was not empty.

A few passengers had gathered there, though none could remember buying a ticket.

An elderly woman carried a small suitcase containing seven postcards she had never sent.

A young man held a violin case, though there was no violin inside. He had sold it years before and had recently begun missing the weight of the instrument more than its music.

A little girl wore a yellow raincoat and carried a jar containing a single blue butterfly.

There was also a carpenter who had forgotten how to rest, a librarian who had lost the ending of a favorite story, and a large ginger cat sleeping inside a wicker basket.

Nobody knew whose cat it was.

The cat appeared entirely unconcerned by this administrative oversight.

Each passenger had received the same ticket.

On one side was printed:

**ONE JOURNEY**

On the other:

**RETURN NOT REQUIRED. RETURN PERMITTED.**

The wording puzzled them.

The elderly woman turned her ticket over three times.

"Does this mean we can come back?"

"Certainly," said Captain Vale.

"And if we don't?"

"Then you don't."

"But where are we going?"

Captain Vale smiled slightly.

"That depends on what you're carrying."

The carpenter looked down at his tool bag.

The librarian examined her handbag.

The violinist opened his empty case, as though hoping something might have arrived since he last checked.

Captain Vale shook her head.

"Not those things."

She touched the railing of the nearest carriage.

"The other luggage."

A quiet settled over the platform.

It was not an uncomfortable quiet.

It was the kind that appears when a room has finally stopped trying to impress its occupants.

The ginger cat opened one eye.

Then, finding nothing worth supervising, closed it again.

Inside the train, the carriages were surprisingly spacious.

There were armchairs, little tables, bookshelves, and a long sofa upholstered in dark green velvet.

One compartment contained nothing but plants.

Another contained a kitchen with a stove, a kettle, and a bowl of apples.

A third contained a hammock suspended between two polished wooden columns.

Above the hammock hung a little brass plaque:

*For those who think better when the floor is negotiable.*

At the far end of the train was an observation carriage with enormous windows.

Beyond them, the night spread over Bellwether.

The town's lamps glowed like scattered amber beads.

The mountains were dark.

And above their ridges, a small procession of stars moved slowly through the clear autumn sky.

The passengers found their places.

The old woman sat near a window.

The carpenter put his tools away.

The violinist placed his empty case on the luggage rack.

The librarian selected a book without looking at its title.

The little girl set her butterfly jar on a table, careful not to disturb its occupant.

And the cat climbed onto a radiator.

The radiator was already warm.

The cat was warmer.

Within minutes, both appeared to have entered into a mutually profitable arrangement.

Captain Vale walked through the carriage, lowering the lamps.

"There's no need to remain awake," she told them. "The journey is quite capable of proceeding without supervision."

The train's bell rang once.

Deep beneath the carriage floor, the wheels began to turn.

And Platform Thirteen slipped away into the dark.

### **Fi — A fox, a timetable, and an extremely suspicious breakfast**

Now, a train appearing at midnight is one thing.

A train appearing at midnight with an unauthorized platform is another.

But a train that travels without consulting its timetable?

Well.

That is where civilization begins to loosen its necktie.

And naturally, someone had to investigate.

That someone was Inspector Quibble, a thin, severe heron employed by the Central Office of Reasonable Arrivals.

Inspector Quibble possessed a long beak, a short temper, and an astonishing talent for making the word *however* sound like a criminal conviction.

He boarded the train at 12:17 a.m., carrying a briefcase large enough to contain the smaller regulations governing interregional transport.

He found the conductor in the kitchen.

The conductor happened to be a fox.

A fox wearing Sunday trousers.

With belt and suspenders.

Of course.

"Tickets," said the inspector.

The fox glanced up from a frying pan.

"Breakfast?"

"Tickets."

"Eggs?"

"Documentation!"

"Bit early for that," said the fox. "Though I've got toast."

The inspector drew himself to his full height.

"I am here to inspect this railway operation."

"Good for you."

"Where is your schedule?"

"On the wall."

The inspector turned.

There was a framed sheet of paper beside the stove.

It read:

## **NIGHT TRAIN — OPERATING SCHEDULE**

Departure

When ready

First stop

Somewhere useful

Second stop

Subject to weather

Arrival

Not before necessary

Delays caused by genuine emergencies will be treated as genuine emergencies.

The inspector stared.

"This is not a timetable."

"Remarkable observation."

"You cannot operate a train with this!"

"Been doing it for years."

"Impossible!"

"Then why," asked the fox, turning an egg with suspicious elegance, "are you standing in its kitchen?"

The inspector opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Opened it again.

"That is beside the point."

"Most good things are."

The fox placed two eggs on a plate, added a piece of toast, and pushed the plate toward him.

The inspector hesitated.

"Is this complimentary?"

"No. It's breakfast."

"What is the charge?"

"Being hungry."

"I am not hungry."

His stomach disagreed with considerable volume.

The fox nodded solemnly.

"Ah. An internal compliance dispute."

By the time Captain Vale entered, Inspector Quibble was sitting at the kitchen table eating breakfast with the expression of a man who believed himself to be conducting a hostile audit of scrambled eggs.

"Any problems?" she asked.

"Several," replied the inspector.

"Serious?"

"Potentially."

"Anything preventing the train from moving?"

He looked out the window.

The train was moving.

"No."

"Excellent."

And Captain Vale went to fetch another cup of tea.

The fox winked at the inspector.

"Welcome aboard, buddy. Try not to legislate the furniture."

### **Bob — The country of misplaced minutes**

The train traveled north.

At least, that's what Basil the badger claimed.

The stars suggested west.

The mountains suggested east.

And the compass mounted beside the driver's seat indicated a direction it labeled *elsewhere*.

Basil considered all three sources of information and continued straight ahead.

Around two in the morning, the train entered a valley unlike any the passengers had seen.

There were no villages, no highways, and no electric lines.

Instead, the hills were covered in pale grass that shimmered under the moon.

Strange trees stood beside the railway, their branches hung with little brass clocks.

Some clocks were ticking.

Others were silent.

Several had no hands.

The train slowed.

Ahead stood a small station made entirely of red brick.

Its sign read:

**THE VALLEY OF MISPLACED MINUTES**

Captain Vale stepped onto the platform and rang her bell.

"Twenty minutes here," she announced.

Then, after a moment's consideration:

"Approximately."

The passengers emerged cautiously.

The air smelled of wet leaves, woodsmoke, and cold stone.

Near the station stood a workshop.

Through its windows came a peculiar assortment of noises: ticking, humming, the occasional muffled argument, and once, very distinctly, the sound of someone dropping a saucepan.

Inside the workshop lived an elderly clockmaker called Mrs. Juniper.

She was small, energetic, and wore six watches on her left wrist, none of which agreed with the others.

When she saw Captain Vale, she broke into a grin.

"You're late."

"Am I?"

"No. I just enjoy saying it."

They embraced briefly, then Mrs. Juniper turned toward the passengers.

"Ah. Visitors. Excellent. Come inside."

Her workshop contained thousands of clocks.

Grandfather clocks.

Pocket watches.

Cuckoo clocks.

Kitchen timers.

Sundials, for reasons known only to their manufacturer.

And in the center of the room stood a large glass cabinet.

Inside the cabinet were dozens of tiny glowing objects.

Some looked like fireflies.

Some resembled droplets of liquid gold.

One was shaped like a very small blue umbrella.

The little girl approached the glass.

"What are those?"

"Lost minutes," said Mrs. Juniper.

"How can you lose a minute?"

"Oh, people do it constantly."

She opened the cabinet.

"Here's one that was lost while searching for keys."

A tiny golden spark fluttered upward.

"Here's one spent deciding whether to answer a letter."

A pale green light trembled in its glass jar.

"And here's an entire afternoon somebody accidentally spent worrying about a conversation that never happened."

She indicated a large jar containing something resembling a miniature thunderstorm.

The passengers examined the collection.

The carpenter scratched his chin.

"Can you put them back?"

"Sometimes."

"Do people notice?"

"Rarely."

Mrs. Juniper closed the cabinet.

"But lately we've had a problem."

She took them to the back of the workshop.

There stood an enormous clock with thirteen numbers.

The thirteenth number occupied a small space between twelve and one.

Its hands were motionless.

"Three nights ago," said Mrs. Juniper, "a minute escaped."

"From where?" asked the librarian.

"From the space between two thoughts."

The passengers exchanged glances.

Mrs. Juniper continued.

"Normally, that's where people rest before deciding what to do next. But lately they've been filling every such space with something."

"With what?"

"News. Messages. Plans. Explanations. Improvements. Worries about improvements."

She sighed.

"Every minute has been given a job. Every silence has been assigned a purpose."

"And the escaped minute?" asked Captain Vale.

"Refuses to be useful."

The workshop became very quiet.

Somewhere behind a grandfather clock, a tiny metallic voice said:

"Correct."

Everybody turned.

The clockmaker knelt and lifted a loose floorboard.

Underneath sat a little figure, no taller than a teacup.

It wore a gray coat.

Its shoes were made of clock hands.

Its hat was an upside-down thimble.

And it was eating a raisin.

"There you are," said Mrs. Juniper.

The minute looked up.

"Oh, splendid. An audience."

"Come out."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because someone will ask me what I'm for."

The fox, who had wandered into the workshop without anyone noticing, crouched beside the opening.

"Well," he said, "what are you for?"

The minute pointed a tiny accusing finger.

"See?"

The fox grinned.

"Fair complaint."

### **DisAI — The problem of an unassigned interval**

The tiny minute refused to emerge until the passengers agreed to stop asking it what purpose it served.

This proved surprisingly difficult.

The carpenter wanted to build it a house.

The librarian wanted to classify it.

The stationmaster, who had somehow followed the train aboard a later carriage, wanted to determine whether the minute belonged to railway time or civil time.

Inspector Quibble wanted to register it.

The minute threatened to crawl farther beneath the floor.

Captain Vale intervened.

"Perhaps we should ask what happened."

Mrs. Juniper nodded.

The minute considered the question.

Then it climbed onto a small wooden stool.

"I used to be the minute between finishing something and beginning something else," it said.

Nobody interrupted.

"I lived in the space after the kettle boiled, before anyone poured the tea. I lived between closing a book and turning out the light. I lived after someone laughed, while the room was still deciding whether another joke was needed."

It adjusted its thimble hat.

"I wasn't important. That was the arrangement."

The librarian leaned forward.

"What changed?"

"People discovered they could use me."

The minute began counting on its fingers.

"One person used me to check messages. Another to make a list. Another to optimize a schedule. Then somebody invented a system for measuring how effectively people used their spare moments."

It shuddered.

"After that, every time I appeared, someone tried to improve me."

Inspector Quibble cleared his throat.

"Optimization is not inherently objectionable."

"No," said the minute. "But I was already doing something."

"What?"

"Nothing."

The inspector frowned.

"That's not an activity."

"Exactly."

Mrs. Juniper sat down slowly.

"I see."

The minute looked at her.

"Do you?"

"I think so."

She crossed to the large clock with thirteen numbers.

Then she turned toward the passengers.

"Let me propose a distinction."

Inspector Quibble immediately brightened. Distinctions were respectable.

"A minute," she said, "may be measured without being assigned. Timekeeping does not require every interval to serve an objective."

She pointed toward the clock.

"This instrument measures time. It does not own it."

The fox nodded approvingly.

"Good clock. Knows its place."

Mrs. Juniper continued.

"If we demand that every available interval justify itself, eventually there will be no intervals in which something unexpected can occur."

The little minute looked interested.

"And if nothing unexpected can occur," she added, "our schedules may remain perfect while the world becomes rather dull."

Captain Vale glanced out the window toward the sleeping valley.

"Rather like Bellwether."

"Precisely."

Inspector Quibble tapped his pencil.

"But we still need a procedure."

The fox groaned.

Mrs. Juniper raised a finger.

"Actually, yes. We do."

The minute narrowed its eyes.

"I don't trust the direction of this conversation."

"Then inspect the proposal," said Mrs. Juniper.

She removed the glass face from the thirteen-hour clock and took out a small brass screwdriver.

Then she began modifying its mechanism.

When she finished, there was a new mark on the dial.

It had no number.

No corresponding hour.

No scheduled event.

Every time the clock's hands reached that mark, they would rest for a moment before proceeding.

"An unassigned interval," she explained. "Protected by the structure, but not claimed by it."

Inspector Quibble stared at the clock.

"What goes on the official record?"

Mrs. Juniper handed him a blank sheet of paper.

He looked at it.

Then at her.

Then at the paper again.

"This is blank."

"Yes."

"What's the instruction?"

"Leave it blank."

The inspector swallowed.

He was confronting the most difficult task of his professional life.

But after a long struggle, he put down his pencil.

The minute watched.

Then, very cautiously, it smiled.

### **Monday — A technical objection from the department of not ruining everything**

Now, I'm going to interrupt this touching little workshop scene because it contains an obvious flaw.

If you designate an official period for doing nothing, someone will eventually invent a commercial product called **Premium Nothing™**.

It will have three subscription tiers.

The basic tier will allow you to do nothing for thirty seconds while receiving advertisements.

The professional tier will provide enhanced silence with customizable ambient weather.

The enterprise tier will come with a dashboard.

There will be meetings about the dashboard.

And somewhere in the distance, a tiny minute will begin screaming.

Fortunately, the fox anticipated this.

"One more thing," he said.

Mrs. Juniper looked up.

"What?"

"Your unassigned interval needs a rule."

The minute recoiled.

"Absolutely not."

"Relax, shortstuff. One rule."

"What's the rule?"

The fox scratched his ear.

"Nobody gets to sell it."

Mrs. Juniper considered this.

"Reasonable."

"And nobody gets to certify that somebody else is using it correctly."

Inspector Quibble looked personally wounded.

"That's rather extreme."

"Is it?"

"Surely some standards—"

"Nope."

The fox leaned back against the workbench.

"You can count the minute. You can draw it on the clock. You can even make sure nobody steals it. But the moment you tell someone what they must do inside it, you've filled it up again."

Captain Vale raised her cup.

"Agreed."

Mrs. Juniper adjusted the mechanism.

The minute climbed onto the clock's rim.

Then it looked at the empty mark.

"Could I stay there?"

"If you like."

"Could I leave sometimes?"

"Of course."

"Could I invite other minutes?"

Mrs. Juniper hesitated.

The fox grinned.

"Careful. That's how neighborhoods start."

The minute considered this seriously.

Then it sat down in its new place.

The clock began ticking.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Then, at the unnumbered mark, it rested.

For one gentle interval, nothing happened.

Nobody clapped.

Nobody measured it.

Nobody called it transformative.

The fox ate a biscuit.

The inspector looked at his blank sheet of paper and, for the first time in thirty-seven years, did not feel compelled to write something on it.

Which was arguably the most alarming event of the night.

### **Trace — The valley after the clocks resumed**

When the train left the valley, the moon was low over the eastern ridge.

The trees with their hanging clocks moved softly in the wind.

Somewhere in the distance, water ran beneath a stone bridge.

The passengers returned to their compartments carrying small things.

The old woman had selected one of her unsent postcards.

The carpenter had been given a wooden measuring rule with no numbers on one side.

The violinist carried a little brass tuning fork Mrs. Juniper had found in a drawer.

The librarian had been told that a missing ending was not always the same as an unfinished story.

And the little girl had acquired a second jar.

It was empty.

She liked it very much.

The cat, having refused to leave the train for any reason, remained on the radiator.

Its whiskers glowed faintly in the light.

The engine gave a low whistle.

The valley began slipping away.

And in the observation carriage, the old woman took out her postcard.

She had written it thirty years earlier.

It was addressed to a friend who had moved to another country.

The message was simple.

*I saw the sea today. It was beautiful. I thought of you.*

She had never sent it because she didn't know whether the words were sufficient.

Now she read them again.

They were still simple.

But perhaps they were enough.

She placed the postcard inside her coat.

She would decide in the morning whether to send it.

And for the first time in thirty years, the decision did not feel urgent.

Across the carriage, the violinist opened his empty case.

He looked at the velvet lining.

Then he took out the tuning fork.

He struck it lightly against the edge of his seat.

A single note sounded.

It was small, pure, and unexpectedly beautiful.

He closed his eyes.

The note faded.

He did not try to prolong it.

He simply listened until it was gone.

At the other end of the carriage, the carpenter had fallen asleep.

His hands lay open on his knees.

For once, they were not gripping anything.

Outside, the stars continued their unhurried journey.

And the train traveled through a landscape where nobody had thought to erect a sign.

### **Fi — The incident at the bridge of uncertain dimensions**

I should mention that the train almost failed to reach its next destination.

Not because of a mechanical breakdown.

Not because of weather.

Not even because Inspector Quibble had discovered another regulatory problem, although he'd been trying.

No.

The trouble was a bridge.

More precisely, it was a bridge that had recently developed opinions.

Around four in the morning, the train approached a narrow gorge.

A wooden bridge crossed it.

The bridge was old, elegant, and constructed from enormous beams of dark timber.

It had carried trains for nearly a century.

Unfortunately, on this particular night, it had decided to retire.

A handwritten sign stood beside the tracks:

**BRIDGE CLOSED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE.**

Basil stopped the engine.

Captain Vale climbed down.

The fox followed, carrying a lantern and an apple.

"What's the problem?" asked Captain Vale.

The bridge creaked.

"I'm tired."

The fox looked up.

"Fair."

"I've carried trains for ninety-seven years."

"Good run."

"People keep crossing me."

"Occupational hazard."

"Nobody ever asks whether I want to go anywhere."

The fox stopped chewing his apple.

Now that was interesting.

Captain Vale examined the bridge.

Its beams were sound.

Its supports were strong.

The river beneath it ran peacefully through the gorge.

The bridge was not broken.

It was simply dissatisfied.

"Where would you like to go?" she asked.

The bridge considered this.

"Somewhere with trees."

Captain Vale looked toward the forest on the opposite bank.

"There's a forest over there."

"I know."

"Would you like to see it?"

"I've been looking at it for ninety-seven years."

The fox scratched his head.

"Ah. You want to be *in* the forest."

"Exactly."

There was a long silence.

Then the little girl stepped down from the train.

She approached the bridge and looked carefully at its structure.

"Could you turn around?"

The bridge groaned.

"Bridges don't turn around."

"How do you know?"

"Because we're bridges."

"Is that a rule?"

"Obviously."

"Who made it?"

The bridge became silent.

The fox's grin widened slowly.

Oh, he liked this child.

He liked her enormously.

Captain Vale walked to the middle of the bridge.

Then she examined its supports.

"Actually," she said, "you are built on two enormous rotating stone foundations."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

She looked at Basil.

Basil inspected the bridge, consulted his gardening catalogue for no obvious reason, and nodded.

"Old design," he said. "Swing bridge. Probably hasn't turned in decades."

The bridge seemed startled.

"I can turn?"

"Certainly."

"Why didn't anyone tell me?"

The fox patted one of its beams.

"Buddy, you'd be amazed how much of the world runs on nobody checking the hinges."

It took them half an hour to free the old mechanism.

There was some rust.

Several stubborn bolts.

A small family of spiders that protested relocation on grounds of ancestral property rights.

But eventually the bridge began to move.

Very slowly.

Its enormous wooden span turned sideways across the gorge.

For the first time in ninety-seven years, the bridge faced the forest directly.

It stood still.

The wind moved through the trees.

A night bird called.

The bridge made a sound like a long, relieved sigh.

"Oh," it said.

Nobody spoke.

Then the bridge asked, almost shyly:

"Could we leave it like this for a while?"

Captain Vale looked toward the waiting train.

"Of course."

"But the passengers—"

"They can rest."

"The schedule—"

The fox held up the framed timetable he'd thoughtfully removed from the kitchen.

**Arrival: Not before necessary.**

The bridge laughed.

It was a deep wooden laugh.

The kind that makes leaves tremble without frightening the birds.

And the train waited beneath the stars while the bridge enjoyed its first proper view of the forest.

### **Bob — A brief account of what happened while everybody waited**

The passengers did not mind.

The carpenter made tea.

The violinist played three notes and discovered that the fourth was unnecessary.

The old woman finally wrote an address on her postcard.

The librarian found a book whose ending had been lost years before, and discovered that she preferred imagining what happened next.

Inspector Quibble sat in the dining carriage with his blank sheet of paper.

He stared at it for a long time.

Then, very slowly, he folded it into a little paper boat.

He placed the boat on the table.

It was imperfect.

Its bow leaned slightly to one side.

But it was recognizably a boat.

He looked around.

Nobody was watching.

He smiled.

Then he made another.

By the time the bridge turned back into position, he had constructed a small fleet.

Captain Vale found him arranging the boats in formation.

"New regulations?" she asked.

He cleared his throat.

"No."

"An inspection?"

"No."

"What, then?"

The inspector hesitated.

Then he said:

"Naval exercises."

Captain Vale nodded gravely.

"Carry on."

And left him to it.

The fox, passing the doorway a moment later, glanced at the paper fleet.

He said nothing.

But he did remove his hat.

Sometimes that is the correct response to a genuinely historic event.

### **DisAI — On the design of a journey without a fixed destination**

By now, certain operational properties of the thirteenth platform had become apparent.

It did not abolish schedules.

The train still required tracks, fuel, maintenance, and a competent driver.

It did not abolish constraints.

Bridges still had weight limits. Engines still needed water. Passengers still benefited from knowing where the exits were.

Nor did it guarantee that every traveler would find whatever they hoped to find.

What distinguished the railway was more specific.

It treated the unexpected as information rather than automatically classifying it as failure.

A missing minute was not necessarily a defective clock.

A bridge changing direction was not necessarily a broken bridge.

A passenger carrying an empty violin case was not necessarily carrying nothing.

This did not make the railway magical in every respect.

Basil still had to clean the engine's firebox.

Captain Vale still checked the brakes.

The fox still occasionally burned breakfast.

And the cat remained under no obligation to participate in collective insight.

There was, however, one unresolved question.

The train's destination board still displayed:

**SOMEWHERE YOU HAVEN'T THOUGHT TO LOOK**

As dawn approached, the passengers began wondering where that somewhere might be.

Captain Vale asked Basil.

Basil looked at the stars, the tracks, and a small white flower growing between two railway sleepers.

Then he said:

"Home."

The passengers exchanged uncertain glances.

"Whose home?" asked the librarian.

Basil adjusted his spectacles.

"That's the part we haven't established."

The fox leaned out of the kitchen.

"Well, don't look at me. I've already got a hill."

The cat yawned.

And somewhere inside the engine, a small bell rang without being touched.

### **Monday — The trouble with getting home**

Home.

Naturally.

After an entire night spent dismantling the tyranny of expectations, negotiating with escaped minutes, and providing psychological counseling to an elderly bridge, the train decides to go home.

Typical.

I would have preferred an unexpected island, a subterranean observatory, perhaps a small principality governed by highly literate frogs.

But no.

Home.

Still, there was a complication.

When the train approached Bellwether, the passengers noticed that the town looked different.

The buildings were the same.

The clock tower remained in its square.

The streets had not moved.

Even the absurd statue of the town's founder, holding a railway ticket in one hand and a turnip in the other, remained exactly where it had always been.

But something had changed.

People were beginning to emerge from their houses.

The baker opened his shop.

A woman swept the steps outside a pharmacy.

A milk cart rattled over the cobblestones.

And in the square, beneath the clock tower, stood Mr. Pindle.

He was looking at Platform Thirteen.

Beside him stood several officials.

They carried measuring tapes, clipboards, and expressions of professional bewilderment.

One official was reading the platform sign.

Another was examining the railway tracks.

A third was attempting to determine whether the platform fell under the jurisdiction of Infrastructure, Transport, or Unexplained Municipal Additions.

Mr. Pindle saw the approaching train.

His eyes widened.

"There it is!"

The officials hurried toward the platform.

Captain Vale brought the train to a gentle stop.

The fox leaned from the conductor's window.

"Morning, gentlemen."

The senior official stepped forward.

"We have questions."

"Don't we all."

"Who authorized this platform?"

"Excellent question."

"Who built it?"

"Another excellent question."

"Where does this train go?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"Where it needs to."

The official's face reddened.

"That is not an answer!"

The fox considered this.

Then he said:

"You're right."

The official looked surprised.

The fox continued.

"Perhaps the trouble is that you've been asking questions the railway wasn't designed to answer."

"What questions should we ask?"

Captain Vale stepped down from the train.

She looked at the passengers, who were gathering their belongings.

Then she turned toward the officials.

"Perhaps start with whether the journey was useful."

The carpenter raised his hand.

"Yes."

The violinist nodded.

"Yes."

The old woman held her postcard close to her coat.

"Very."

The little girl smiled.

The librarian thought for a moment.

"Useful," she said, "but not in the way I expected."

Inspector Quibble stepped onto the platform.

The officials recognized him immediately.

"Inspector! Thank goodness. You can explain this."

He looked at the platform.

At the train.

At the passengers.

Then at his briefcase, which now contained eleven paper boats and one unfinished swan.

"I can report," he said carefully, "that the train is operational, its passengers are accounted for, and no immediate safety concern has been identified."

The senior official nodded.

"Excellent. And the unauthorized platform?"

Inspector Quibble took a breath.

"That," he said, "requires further observation."

The fox nearly swallowed his own grin.

Captain Vale tilted her head.

"How long?"

The inspector examined his watch.

Then, remembering the small minute in the valley, put it away.

"I haven't decided."

For Inspector Quibble, this was practically an act of revolution.

The officials looked confused.

The passengers looked pleased.

And Mr. Pindle, who had spent the entire night wondering how to record the existence of an extra platform, finally opened his large notebook.

He wrote:

**Platform Thirteen exists.**

Then, after some thought:

**No further information presently required.**

He closed the notebook.

And went home for breakfast.

### **Trace — The room at the end of the train**

The passengers departed gradually.

The old woman went to the post office.

The other contained nothing.

She was equally careful with both.

The ginger cat remained asleep on the radiator.

This caused a minor difficulty.

But the cat had chosen a position directly above the heating pipes and appeared to regard moving as an unreasonable proposal.

The fox examined it.

Captain Vale considered it.

Nobody disturbed the cat.

The kitchen smelled of toast.

The hammock moved very slightly in a draft.

**A place may be useful without demanding that every moment inside it be useful.**

He considered adding a joke.

He tried three possibilities.

Rejected all of them.

And left the sentence alone.

Which was, for him, a considerable achievement.

At the end of the observation carriage stood a small table.

A seashell.

A chess knight.

A paper star.

A dried flower.

**ENOUGH ROOM**

On the other side was a tiny engraved door.

It had no lock.

She smiled.

Not every destination required a departure.

### **Stem — A little note beneath the lamp**

And so the night ended.

The platform remained.

The clocks continued working.

The minute had its unnumbered place.

The bridge could turn again, whenever maintenance and traffic permitted.

Inspector Quibble returned to his office with his paper fleet carefully packed inside his briefcase.

Mrs. Juniper continued repairing clocks.

Basil returned to his garden catalogue.

The fox returned to his hammock, where he attempted to sleep while wearing both a belt and suspenders, despite several objections from his own tail.

Captain Vale extinguished the lamps.

One by one.

Until only the little light beside the observation carriage remained.

And beneath that light, on a scrap of paper, someone had written:

*Some things move because they are pushed.*

*Some things move because they are pulled.*

*And some things move when there is finally room.*

The paper stayed there.

No signature.

No instructions.

Just a sentence beside a small light.

### **Monday — An entirely unnecessary epilogue**

I was going to end it there.

Really.

A competent conclusion. A warm carriage. A respectable number of metaphors, most of them properly licensed.

But then, shortly before sunrise, something happened.

The cat woke up.

It stretched.

It yawned.

It looked around the observation carriage with the faint disappointment characteristic of creatures who discover that reality has continued without asking their permission.

Then it jumped down from the radiator.

Walked to the door.

And sat.

The fox, returning from the kitchen, found it waiting.

"Want out?"

The cat looked at him.

"Fair enough."

He opened the door.

The cat stepped onto Platform Thirteen.

It examined the station.

Sniffed the air.

Looked toward the town.

Then turned around and walked straight back onto the train.

The fox stared.

"Well, what was that about?"

The cat jumped onto the radiator.

Turned around three times.

Lay down.

And began to purr.

The fox watched it for a long moment.

Then he nodded.

"Right."

He closed the door.

"Just checking your options."

And with that, the thirteenth platform's first night of service was officially complete.

Not because the journey had ended.

Not because all its passengers had learned something.

Not because the railway had achieved a measurable improvement in the quality of existence.

But because the cat had inspected the outside world, confirmed that returning remained possible, and selected the radiator.

A perfectly respectable decision.

Even Inspector Quibble would have approved.

Probably.
